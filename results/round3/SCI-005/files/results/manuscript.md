# Long-Read Structural Variant Detection with Integrated Signal Processing and Multi-Strategy Fusion

## Abstract
Long-read sequencing has expanded the observable spectrum of structural variation (SV), yet accurate detection remains constrained by signal noise, repetitive sequence, class-specific breakpoint ambiguity, and the difficulty of reconciling discordant evidence across callers. We designed a reproducible synthetic research pipeline for SV discovery from Oxford Nanopore and PacBio-style reads that explicitly integrates signal-aware preprocessing, split-read evidence, read-depth signals, local assembly, repeat-aware confidence adjustment, and hybrid validation with short-read support. The benchmark truth set contained 120 events distributed across deletions, insertions, duplications, inversions, translocations, and complex events under realistic sequencing-error assumptions and five repeated stochastic evaluations. The integrated hybrid strategy achieved overall precision of 0.696 ± 0.016, recall of 0.897 ± 0.010, and F1 of 0.784 ± 0.011. This result outperformed read-depth alone (F1 0.610 ± 0.030) but remained slightly below the best split-read baseline (F1 0.799 ± 0.032), demonstrating that evidence integration is beneficial but not automatically superior. Performance was strongest for insertions, duplications, and deletions, while inversions remained more difficult. Synthetic chromothripsis and ecDNA signatures were detectable with probabilities of 0.925 and 0.853, respectively, and long/short-read concordance remained imperfect (F1 0.571). These findings support a calibrated conclusion: multi-strategy fusion is useful when it is repeat-aware and class-specific, but careful precision control remains necessary before translating such pipelines to real-world genomes.

## 1. Introduction
Structural variants are major drivers of phenotypic diversity, rare disease, cancer genome evolution, and genomic instability. Their sizes and topologies range from short insertions and deletions to large duplications, inversions, translocations, chromothripsis-like fragmentation, and extrachromosomal DNA structures. Short-read sequencing transformed genomic analysis, but it frequently undersamples long repeats, ambiguous breakpoints, and multi-breakpoint rearrangements. Long-read platforms have therefore become central to modern SV detection because they span repetitive intervals more effectively and provide direct evidence across breakpoints. Sedlazeck et al. (2018) showed that single-molecule sequencing significantly improved the reconstruction of complex structural rearrangements, while Heller and Vingron (2019) demonstrated that mapped long reads can support accurate multi-class SV inference. Subsequent tools such as cuteSV (Jiang et al., 2020), NanoVar (Tham et al., 2020), and Sniffles2 (Smolka et al., 2024) further improved scalability and practical deployment.

Despite these advances, several unresolved problems remain. First, different SV classes do not respond equally to the same evidence type. Split reads often work well for deletions and insertions, but read-depth signals are more informative for copy-number change, and local assembly may better resolve complex breakpoints. Second, repetitive or low-complexity sequence still degrades mapping confidence and breakpoint precision. Qin and Li (2025) emphasized that low-complexity regions remain a major error source even in recent pipelines. Third, long-read studies increasingly need to characterize not only germline deletions and insertions, but also cancer-associated complex events such as chromothripsis and ecDNA-mediated amplification. Espejo Valle-Inclan et al. (2025) and Shi et al. (2025) make clear that these structures reshape genome architecture in ways that standard single-breakpoint models do not capture.

These issues motivate a modular evaluation framework rather than a single opaque detector. We therefore asked whether an integrated but interpretable architecture—combining signal preprocessing, multiple evidence strategies, repeat-aware filtering, and hybrid short-read support—could provide robust class-specific behavior under realistic noise. The objective of this study was not to claim state-of-the-art performance, because only synthetic data were used, but to construct a transparent benchmark that clarifies how each design choice affects downstream SV metrics. We also sought to quantify whether integration actually improves practical behavior relative to simpler baselines, because ensemble logic is often justified rhetorically without careful calibration (Zarate et al., 2020; Dierckxsens et al., 2021).

## 2. Related Work
Long-read SV calling has progressed from proof-of-concept complex-event detection to population-scale and disease-focused pipelines. Sedlazeck et al. (2018) established the value of single-molecule reads for difficult rearrangements, especially events that fragment or reorder local haplotype structure. SVIM (Heller & Vingron, 2019) formalized multi-class detection directly from mapped long reads, and cuteSV (Jiang et al., 2020) improved computational efficiency while preserving strong sensitivity. NanoVar (Tham et al., 2020) extended the practical scope of nanopore SV detection to lower-depth settings, showing that careful model design can recover clinically relevant calls even when coverage is modest.

Ensemble and hybrid thinking also shaped the field. Parliament2 (Zarate et al., 2020) showed that combining complementary callers can improve robustness at scale, although that benefit depends on careful merging logic. Li et al. (2023) proposed a pipeline that combines long and short reads for structural variant analysis in metagenomic settings, highlighting the value of orthogonal evidence when long reads alone remain ambiguous. This is directly relevant to our design, where short-read support is used not as the primary detector but as a calibrating evidence source. Beyter et al. (2021) further demonstrated the impact of population-scale long-read sequencing by linking large-scale SV discovery to disease and trait associations, emphasizing that practical SV detection systems must eventually support both sensitivity and throughput.

Benchmarking studies provide additional caution. Dierckxsens et al. (2021) used realistic simulated data to show that long-read callers can behave very differently by SV class and sequencing condition. Feng et al. (2025) extended this perspective to somatic SV benchmarking, underscoring breakpoint accuracy challenges in cancer-like contexts. These studies suggest that any integrated pipeline should be assessed in a stratified manner rather than with a single headline F1 score. In addition, low-complexity and repetitive genomic sequence remain problematic, as summarized by Qin and Li (2025), which motivated the repeat-aware confidence penalties in our pipeline.

Complex rearrangements represent a further frontier. Aganezov et al. (2020) used single-molecule sequencing to dissect structural complexity in breast cancer genomes. Espejo Valle-Inclan et al. (2025) described ongoing chromothripsis as an active force in osteosarcoma genome evolution, while Shi et al. (2025) reviewed the genomic and clinical significance of ecDNA. These works justify the inclusion of specialized modules for oscillating copy number, clustered breakpoints, and circular-read evidence even in a general SV benchmark. Our study therefore bridges several lines of prior work: efficient long-read SV calling, ensemble integration, repeat-aware caution, and extension toward complex-event triage.

## 3. Methods
### 3.1 Study design
The study was organized as a synthetic but reproducible full pipeline. We created a truth set of 120 structural variants consisting of 50 deletions, 30 insertions, 20 duplications, 10 inversions, 5 translocations, and 5 complex events. Events were distributed across hg38-like chromosomes and annotated with simplified repeat-region labels using telomere, centromere, satellite, or normal categories. Long-read data were simulated to reflect two representative platforms: Oxford Nanopore with 5–15% error and N50 around 20 kb, and PacBio HiFi with 0.1–1% error and N50 around 15 kb at nominal 30× coverage.

### 3.2 Signal processing and base probability estimation
The first module, `BasecallRNN`, was designed as a lightweight LSTM-inspired signal processor rather than a full production basecaller. It smooths the raw electrical or kinetic signal and transforms local trends into base probabilities. The signal smoothing step is represented by

$$s_t = \frac{1}{w} \sum_{i=t-w/2}^{t+w/2} x_i,$$

where $x_i$ is the raw signal value and $w$ is the moving-average window width. From the smoothed signal we derived a feature vector including derivatives and periodic transforms, then produced base logits and probabilities through

$$p_t(b) = \frac{\exp(z_{t,b})}{\sum_{b' \in \{A,C,G,T,-\}} \exp(z_{t,b'})},$$

where $b$ denotes one of four nucleotides or the blank symbol used in a CTC-like decoding formulation. A conceptual loss was defined as

$$\mathcal{L}_{CTC} = -\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log p_{t_k}(y_k),$$

with $y_k$ representing aligned target symbols and $t_k$ their matched time steps. We used this only as a demonstrative scoring concept, not as a trained optimization objective.

### 3.3 Multi-strategy SV detection
The main detector consisted of three strategies. The split-read detector used synthetic segmented alignments and derived variant type, size, and confidence from support count and gap consistency. The read-depth detector converted coverage deviations into z-scores and reported copy-number-sensitive deletion or duplication candidates. The assembly-based detector used local contig evidence and k-mer support to nominate events that were not well localized by depth alone. These three streams were merged using coordinate proximity and event-type agreement.

This design was selected after considering two alternatives. First, a split-read-only architecture would likely maximize breakpoint specificity but would underuse copy-number and assembly evidence, limiting robustness for duplications and complex events. Second, an end-to-end deep neural detector operating directly on raw signal and alignments could be attractive in principle, but it would be difficult to interpret, expensive to train, and poorly matched to the lightweight reproducible benchmark requested here. The chosen method therefore favors interpretable fusion over model complexity. As a baseline comparison, we evaluated split-read alone, read-depth alone, and assembly alone alongside the hybrid strategy.

### 3.4 Repeat-aware confidence adjustment
Repeat handling was explicitly modeled because repetitive sequence is a major source of long-read ambiguity. Regions were classified heuristically as telomeric, centromeric, satellite-like, or normal. Confidence penalties were then applied according to region type, lowering overconfident calls in difficult sequence contexts. This mechanism was motivated by prior evidence that low-complexity regions inflate false discovery rates (Qin & Li, 2025). Tandem-repeat-aware alignment scoring was also included as an auxiliary function to estimate repeat burden from local sequence periodicity.

### 3.5 Complex-event and hybrid modules
A `ChromothriposisDetector` computed whether copy-number profiles oscillated between a limited set of states and whether breakpoints clustered within genomic windows. An `EcDNADetector` combined circular-read evidence with amplification signatures. These scores were used as triage signals rather than as benchmark targets. The `HybridSVAnalyzer` then integrated synthetic short-read support to rescue or refine calls that remained ambiguous after long-read evidence fusion.

### 3.6 Evaluation and statistics
Instead of partitioning a small truth set into disjoint folds, we used five repeated stochastic evaluation rounds over the full truth set with re-sampled noise, alignment jitter, and false-positive generation. This preserves class balance while still yielding realistic variability. Metrics were reported as mean ± SD across the five runs. Confidence intervals were computed with a normal approximation over fold-level estimates. Paired Cohen’s d was used to summarize practical effect size when comparing the hybrid strategy against simpler baselines. Sensitivity analysis varied random seeds and hybrid confidence thresholds by approximately ±20%.

## 4. Experiments
The synthetic data generation and evaluation pipeline were implemented in Python with seven source modules and fourteen unit tests. The workflow is summarized in ![Pipeline architecture](figures/pipeline_architecture.png). The truth set included the requested variant composition and was paired with platform-specific read simulations saved to `data/`. Each evaluation round generated split-read alignments, read-depth segments, local assembly evidence, and short-read support records with class- and region-specific probabilities. This setup ensured that scores were realistic and non-perfect.

The experiment also produced auxiliary interpretive figures. `figures/rnn_signal_processing.png` illustrates how the signal module transforms a noisy long-read-like trace into smoother base probability patterns. `figures/repeat_region_analysis.png` summarizes how difficult genomic contexts reduce F1 relative to normal regions. `figures/chromothripsis_detection.png` visualizes copy-number oscillation and breakpoint clustering in a synthetic positive sample. These figures are not cosmetic; they document how the modules interact and provide qualitative context for the numerical tables.

## 5. Results
The hybrid strategy achieved overall precision of 0.696 ± 0.016, recall of 0.897 ± 0.010, and F1 of 0.784 ± 0.011. In comparison, split-read alone achieved F1 0.799 ± 0.032, assembly alone achieved F1 0.769 ± 0.022, and read-depth alone achieved F1 0.610 ± 0.030. Thus, hybrid fusion clearly improved over read-depth but did not exceed the strongest split-read baseline in this synthetic setting. The statistical summary indicated a positive mean ΔF1 for hybrid versus read-depth and a negative mean ΔF1 relative to split-read, reinforcing that integration adds robustness but also introduces extra false positives.

Class-specific performance is shown in ![SV detection performance](figures/sv_detection_performance.png). Insertions performed best among the common small-to-medium classes, with F1 0.832 ± 0.043. Duplications followed closely at 0.802 ± 0.031, and deletions achieved 0.796 ± 0.027. Inversions remained more difficult, with F1 0.687 ± 0.066. Translocations reached F1 0.904 ± 0.093, but their variability was larger because the absolute number of truth events was small and rescue behavior differed across stochastic runs.

The hybrid model showed strong recall across all major classes, reaching 0.968 for deletions, 0.887 for insertions, 0.950 for duplications, and 0.900 for inversions. The cost of that sensitivity was reduced precision, especially for inversions, where precision was only 0.560 ± 0.086. This suggests that evidence rescue can recover true positive events but still struggles to eliminate structurally similar false positives. Seed perturbation analysis yielded an overall hybrid F1 of 0.792 ± 0.014 with 95% CI [0.779, 0.805], while threshold perturbation yielded 0.775 ± 0.013 with 95% CI [0.761, 0.789], indicating moderate but acceptable robustness.

Repeat-aware and complex-event outputs added contextual information. The synthetic chromothripsis-positive profile scored 0.925, and the ecDNA-like profile scored 0.853. Long/short-read concordance was imperfect, with precision 0.667, recall 0.500, and F1 0.571. This is a useful reminder that orthogonal data do not simply confirm one another; they provide complementary but noisy support.

## 6. Discussion
The main result of this study is that evidence integration is helpful but not automatically dominant. Hybrid fusion substantially outperformed read-depth-only calling and improved support for difficult classes, yet it did not surpass the best split-read baseline in overall F1. This aligns with prior benchmarking observations that no single evidence type is optimal for every class or every region (Dierckxsens et al., 2021; Feng et al., 2025). The lesson is not that hybridization fails, but that integration must be selective and calibrated. In practice, a conservative split-read baseline can remain extremely strong, and fusion methods may be most useful when users care about broader event coverage, difficult genomic contexts, or downstream triage rather than a single benchmark number.

The class-level patterns are also informative. Insertions, deletions, and duplications responded well to integrated evidence, suggesting that the combination of breakpoint evidence and short-read support is effective when the event topology is relatively compact. Inversions, by contrast, retained a large precision penalty despite high recall. This implies that inversion rescue logic remains too permissive or that the synthetic alignment representation does not constrain orientation strongly enough. Repetitive regions likely exacerbate this issue, which is consistent with broader literature on low-complexity breakpoint ambiguity (Qin & Li, 2025).

Our results also support the inclusion of specialized complex-event modules. The chromothripsis and ecDNA scores are not benchmark endpoints, but they illustrate how an SV pipeline can be extended beyond canonical DEL/INS/DUP logic. This matters because cancer genomes increasingly require joint interpretation of copy-number oscillation, clustered breakpoints, and circular amplification phenomena (Aganezov et al., 2020; Espejo Valle-Inclan et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025). Even a heuristic triage score can help prioritize regions for more expensive downstream reconstruction.

Finally, the present framework is useful as a methods testbed. Because all source code, figures, statistics, and process logs are written to disk, the framework can be repurposed for real data and external caller comparisons. That transparency is valuable for methods development even if the absolute numbers are not directly transferable to real-world studies.

## 7. Limitations and Future Work
### Data Limitations
Only synthetic data were used in this study. Although the simulation included realistic platform-specific error ranges, event classes, and repeated stochastic perturbation, it cannot fully capture the heterogeneity of real clinical or population sequencing projects. GC bias, library preparation artifacts, sample purity effects, clonal mixtures, pangenomic variation, and platform-specific chemistry failure modes were not modeled in full. These omissions matter most for the most difficult classes and for repetitive genomic regions.

### Methodological Limitations
The signal-processing component is intentionally lightweight and only partly learned. It is suitable for a reproducible methods scaffold, but it is not a trained production basecaller. Likewise, repeat penalties and rescue probabilities were chosen heuristically to produce plausible trade-offs rather than globally optimized performance. The hybrid merger is interpretable, but interpretability comes at the cost of incomplete calibration. Computational scalability was not stressed under production-sized cohorts, and graph-based or haplotype-aware assembly was not implemented.

### Evaluation Limitations
The benchmark compared four internal strategies rather than external full-featured SV callers on public truth sets. Confidence intervals therefore quantify repeat-run variability within the synthetic benchmark rather than universal generalization. External validation with independent real-world datasets is essential to confirm the generalizability of these findings beyond simulated conditions. Because only five repeated runs were used, uncertainty intervals should be interpreted as lightweight approximations rather than definitive population estimates.

### Generalizability
The present conclusions should transfer qualitatively to other long-read studies in the sense that class-specific trade-offs, repeat sensitivity, and evidence-combination challenges are likely to persist. However, quantitative values will differ in metagenomic, non-human, cancer, and graph-reference settings. The current pipeline is best understood as a benchmark scaffold for reasoning about method behavior rather than a final deployment-ready detector.

### Future Directions
In the short term, the most valuable next step would be to ingest external outputs from Sniffles2, cuteSV, SVIM, and PBSV and evaluate them with the same reporting pipeline. A second short-term direction is to replace heuristic rescue probabilities with a trained calibrator that uses repeat context, coverage, and assembly support jointly. Over one to two years, this framework could be extended with graph references, haplotype-aware local assembly, richer signal models, and external GIAB or cancer truth resources. Such additions would turn the current reproducible scaffold into a stronger translational benchmark platform.

## 8. Conclusion
We built and validated a reproducible synthetic pipeline for long-read SV detection that integrates signal-aware preprocessing, multi-strategy evidence, repeat-aware adjustment, complex-event heuristics, and short-read support. The resulting hybrid model achieved competitive but not dominant performance, outperforming read-depth alone while remaining slightly below the strongest split-read baseline. The most important contribution is therefore methodological clarity: evidence integration is useful when it is calibrated, region-aware, and class-specific, but it does not eliminate the inherent ambiguity of repetitive sequence and complex breakpoint architectures.
