# A Machine Learning Framework for Molecular Design of Environmentally-Controlled Biodegradable Polymers: Integrating Hydrolysis Kinetics, Enzymatic Degradation, and Marine Environment Simulation

## Abstract
Biodegradable polymer design is frequently discussed as a sustainability problem, yet most computational studies still isolate only one layer of the design space: environmental proxies, binary biodegradability classification, or enzyme-specific degradation assays. To address that fragmentation, this work develops a unified computational framework that integrates hydrolysis-rate prediction, mechanical-property tradeoff analysis, Michaelis-Menten enzymatic degradation, marine-environment simulation, combinatorial copolymer search, and polymer-specific case studies within a single reproducible Python workflow. A synthetic hydrolysis dataset of 300 polymer samples was generated from a physically constrained rate equation spanning bond identity, crystallinity, molecular weight, hydrophilicity, temperature, and pH. Four regressors were benchmarked with 5-fold cross-validation. Support vector regression delivered the strongest hydrolysis performance with cross-validated R² = 0.925 ± 0.037 and 95% CI [0.878, 0.971], while Linear Regression remained competitive at 0.924 ± 0.025, indicating that a noisy mechanistic prior can stabilize both linear and non-linear models. A surrogate-based mechanical-design study on 200 formulations identified 39 Pareto-optimal candidates balancing degradation rate against normalized tensile and modulus performance. Michaelis-Menten simulations indicated that Cutinase was the most effective enzyme for PHA under the chosen operating conditions, reaching 56.58% degraded fraction after 30 days. Marine simulations further showed that surface-zone PHA approached complete mass loss after one year, with a Monte Carlo mean of 99.9999996897641% and 95% CI [99.9999971352870, 99.9999999999966]. In the combinatorial design module, PHA-co-PBS at polymer-A fraction 1.00 achieved the highest desirability score (0.800), whereas the case-study analysis suggested that crystallinity reduction was a more efficient route to accelerate degradation than indiscriminate molecular-weight reduction. These findings demonstrate a practical route from mechanistic reasoning toward rate-aware, environment-aware molecular design of biodegradable polymers.

## 1. Introduction
Biodegradable polymers are increasingly positioned as a cornerstone of sustainable materials development because they potentially decouple application-specific performance from long-term environmental persistence. Reviews of the field emphasize that this promise is meaningful only when biodegradation pathways, processing requirements, mechanical integrity, and end-of-life scenarios are jointly considered (Kim et al., 2023; Rosenboom et al., 2022; Samir et al., 2022). In practice, however, research workflows remain fragmented. Polymer chemists often optimize thermal and mechanical properties first, biodegradation biologists study enzyme-substrate interactions separately, and environmental scientists investigate marine persistence using proxy descriptors rather than integrated kinetic models. As a result, the community still lacks a compact computational framework that connects molecular design variables to degradation rates under realistic environmental conditions.

Several influential studies frame the current state of the art. Min et al. (2020) ranked degradation tendencies of marine plastic debris from physical and molecular descriptors, establishing an important link between structure and environmental fate. Yet their framework was proxy-oriented and did not explicitly model enzymatic turnover or dynamic degradation rates. Fransen et al. (2023) advanced the field by combining high-throughput experimentation with machine learning and reporting biodegradability classification accuracy above 82% for 642 polyesters. That result is highly valuable for screening, but classification alone cannot directly answer how quickly a candidate will hydrolyze, how much mechanical performance must be sacrificed to accelerate degradation, or whether a material behaves differently in coastal versus deep-sea environments. Baldera-Moreno et al. (2022) reviewed mathematical models for controlled biodegradation, but did not integrate those mechanistic insights with modern machine-learning surrogates and design-search workflows.

This study addresses those limitations by proposing a modular computational framework that unifies six analysis components: hydrolysis-rate prediction, multi-objective mechanical tradeoff analysis, Michaelis-Menten enzyme kinetics, marine-environment simulation, combinatorial copolymer design, and polymer-specific modification case studies. The framework is centered on three widely discussed biodegradable polymers—PLA, PHA, and PBS—because they span different balances of crystallinity, stiffness, and degradability and are strongly represented in the sustainability literature (Barletta et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2023; Samir et al., 2022). The objective is not to claim experimental replacement, but to create a reproducible computational scaffold that can support hypothesis generation, ranking of experimental priorities, and translation from molecular descriptors to application-relevant design decisions.

The proposed approach is distinct in scope. Rather than stopping at abiotic ranking, binary classification, or isolated enzyme simulation, it links all six aspects of polymer molecular design in one pipeline. This broader coupling matters because biodegradable-polymer design is fundamentally a constrained optimization problem: materials must persist long enough in service, then degrade rapidly enough after disposal, while remaining manufacturable and compatible with relevant environmental conditions. By explicitly framing the problem in those terms, the present work seeks to provide a more actionable bridge between prior literature and practical polymer design.

## 2. Related Work
Recent literature on biodegradable polymers can be organized into four overlapping themes: broad materials reviews, machine-learning-driven screening, enzymatic and biochemical degradation, and environment-specific degradation modeling. The broadest context is provided by reviews such as Kim et al. (2023), Rosenboom et al. (2022), and Samir et al. (2022), which map the chemistry, application space, and sustainability tradeoffs of biodegradable plastics. These reviews collectively show that the field is no longer limited to replacing petroleum-derived plastics with nominally degradable analogs; instead, successful materials must be tuned for processing, use-phase durability, recyclability, and degradation context. Barletta et al. (2022) gives a detailed example of this broader design problem in PBS, highlighting how processing routes and industrial requirements interact with degradation-related behavior.

Machine learning is increasingly used to accelerate polymer discovery. Fransen et al. (2023) demonstrated that high-throughput experimentation combined with predictive models can classify biodegradable polyesters with strong performance, illustrating the value of data-driven screening in polymer science. Malashin et al. (2025) further reviewed support vector machines in polymer research and argued that SVMs remain highly competitive when datasets are modest, descriptors are structured, and interpretability is still needed. These studies motivate the inclusion of classical ML baselines such as SVR, Random Forests, and Gradient Boosting in the present work. However, neither the Fransen et al. (2023) classification setting nor the method-centric review by Malashin et al. (2025) provides a full design framework that couples degradation rate prediction with environmental simulation and multi-objective optimization.

Enzymatic degradation is a second major theme. Tournier et al. (2023) reviewed enzyme-enabled plastic degradation and emphasized the need to connect catalytic efficiency, polymer accessibility, and operating conditions. Knott et al. (2020) showed that engineered two-enzyme systems can substantially improve depolymerization performance, while Orlando et al. (2023) described the biotechnology ecosystem needed for circular plastic valorization. Penas et al. (2024) added polymer-specific evidence by studying self-degradable enzyme-embedded polyester systems, illustrating that molecular design and enzyme availability cannot be treated independently. These studies collectively justify the explicit inclusion of Michaelis-Menten kinetics in a polymer-design workflow rather than treating biodegradation as a purely phenomenological property.

Environment-specific degradation modeling remains less integrated. Min et al. (2020) provided an influential ranking framework for marine debris, but their method relied primarily on physical-property proxies and not on a dynamic rate model with biochemical coupling. Baldera-Moreno et al. (2022) summarized mathematical biodegradation models under controlled conditions, but their review stops short of a practical ML-mechanistic design workflow. The present study therefore draws on both strands: it adopts mechanistic rate expressions to preserve physical plausibility and wraps them with surrogate modeling, uncertainty quantification, and candidate ranking. In doing so, it also responds to the broader circular-economy perspective articulated by Rosenboom et al. (2022), which requires materials decisions to be informed by both performance and end-of-life outcomes.

## 3. Methods
The framework was implemented as seven Python modules: six scientific-analysis modules and one orchestration script. The workflow begins with a hydrolysis-rate generator and benchmarker, then propagates its logic into mechanical tradeoff analysis, enzyme kinetics, ocean simulation, combinatorial copolymer search, and case-study comparisons. All modules use lightweight dependencies only: numpy, pandas, scipy, scikit-learn, and matplotlib. Random seeds were fixed to 42 within the source modules to support reproducibility, and all outputs were saved to versionable files in `results/`, `figures/`, and `data/`.

### 3.1 Hydrolysis-rate model
The hydrolysis module generated 300 synthetic polymer samples. Each sample comprised bond type (ester, anhydride, carbonate, amide), crystallinity fraction, molecular weight, hydrophilicity, temperature, and pH. The target degradation rate was defined by a physically motivated equation:

$$
k = k_{base}(b) \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{ref}}\right)\right) \cdot 10^{a(pH-7)} \cdot \frac{1}{1+bX_c} \cdot \left(\frac{MW}{100000}\right)^{-1/2}
$$

where $k_{base}(b)$ encodes bond chemistry, $E_a$ is activation energy, $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is absolute temperature, $T_{ref}$ is the reference temperature, $X_c$ is crystallinity, and $MW$ is molecular weight. Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.1k was added so that the benchmark would remain realistic rather than degenerate. A noisy mechanistic prior (`physics_prior`) was also derived by perturbing the noiseless physical rate with 30% relative noise. This prior was intentionally imperfect: it gives the models access to a physically informed feature without leaking the exact target.

Four regressors were trained after an 80/20 train-test split: Linear Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and SVR with an RBF kernel. The method choice was deliberate. Linear Regression was kept as the simplest interpretable baseline. Random Forest and Gradient Boosting were chosen because they are strong tabular-data baselines with moderate computational cost. SVR was included because polymer datasets often remain small to medium-sized and SVM-based methods are still competitive in that setting (Malashin et al., 2025). Deep neural networks were considered but rejected: with only 300 synthetic samples, their additional flexibility would likely offer little practical benefit while reducing interpretability and reproducibility.

### 3.2 Mechanical-degradation tradeoff model
The mechanical module generated 200 synthetic formulation records with crystallinity, molecular weight, plasticizer content, filler content, and comonomer ratio as inputs. Tensile strength, elastic modulus, and degradation rate were synthesized from physically interpretable algebraic rules plus Gaussian noise. Three GradientBoostingRegressor surrogates were then fit—one for each target property. We selected gradient boosting here because the targets depend on non-linear interactions, but the sample size remains modest and does not justify heavy optimization packages.

A lightweight NSGA-II-style random search was used instead of full evolutionary optimization software. Specifically, 1000 candidate formulations were sampled across the feasible domain, their properties were predicted with the surrogates, and the non-dominated set was extracted in the two-dimensional objective space of mechanical score and degradation rate. This strategy was selected over Bayesian optimization because the study goal was to visualize the Pareto structure rather than to minimize the number of objective evaluations. A normalized mechanical score was defined as the sum of normalized tensile strength and elastic modulus, which provides a transparent compromise between stiffness and strength.

### 3.3 Michaelis-Menten enzymatic degradation
The enzyme module modeled substrate depletion using classical Michaelis-Menten kinetics:

$$
v = \frac{V_{max}[S]}{K_m + [S]}
$$

$$
\frac{d[S]}{dt} = -\frac{V_{max}[S]}{K_m + [S]}
$$

The effective maximum velocity was further modulated by temperature and pH:

$$
V_{max}(T,pH) = V_{max,0} \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{ref}}\right)\right) \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{pH-pH_{opt}}{w}\right)^2\right)
$$

Four enzymes (Lipase, Esterase, Cutinase, PETase) were simulated against three polymers (PBS, PLA, PHA) over 30 days with `odeint`. These enzymes were selected because they span realistic differences in catalytic ceiling, affinity, pH preference, and activation energy, reflecting the enzymatic landscape discussed by Knott et al. (2020), Tournier et al. (2023), Orlando et al. (2023), and Penas et al. (2024). More complex surface-limited or product-inhibited kinetics were considered, but the classical form was preferred here because the aim was to expose interpretable dependencies rather than build a highly parameterized enzyme model.

### 3.4 Ocean-environment simulation
The ocean module simulated PLA, PHA, and PBS degradation in four environmental zones: surface waters, mesopelagic waters, deep sea, and coastal/littoral waters. The total degradation constant was modeled as

$$
k_{total} = k_{hydrolysis}(T,pH) \cdot f_{microbial}(A) \cdot f_{UV}(z)
$$

with mass loss computed as

$$
\mathrm{Mass\ loss}(t) = \left(1 - e^{-k_{total}t}\right) \times 100
$$

where microbial activity was represented by a simple multiplicative factor and UV exposure was elevated for the surface zone only. The structure intentionally extends the proxy ranking logic of Min et al. (2020) into a dynamic time-series representation. A 200-sample Monte Carlo analysis for surface-zone PHA introduced uncertainty in temperature (±2 °C) and pH (±0.1) to produce 95% confidence intervals.

### 3.5 Combinatorial design and case studies
The combinatorial module enumerated 63 copolymer compositions across PLA-co-PHA, PLA-co-PBS, and PHA-co-PBS systems. Thermal and mechanical properties were interpolated from literature-based endpoint values with weak interaction parameters and small Gaussian perturbations. A desirability score balanced normalized degradation rate, normalized tensile strength, and proximity of Tg to room temperature. This approach was chosen over exhaustive molecular simulation because the design objective was comparative screening, not atomistic accuracy.

The case-study module then examined three intervention classes for each base polymer: molecular-weight reduction, crystallinity reduction, and degradable-monomer copolymerization. Degradation rate and tensile strength were recalculated for each modification. We define modification efficiency as the increase in degradation rate divided by the associated loss in tensile strength. This metric is admittedly simplified, but it provides an interpretable scalar for prioritizing laboratory interventions.

### 3.6 Method selection rationale
The framework deliberately uses classical machine learning and low-order mechanistic equations instead of heavier deep-learning pipelines. Two alternative strategies were considered and rejected. First, an end-to-end deep neural regressor could in principle learn hidden interactions among polymer descriptors, but the dataset size in this project is too small to justify such model complexity, and the resulting parameters would be difficult to interpret chemically. Second, a purely analytical approach based only on closed-form hydrolysis expressions would be chemically elegant, but it would fail to support multi-objective surrogate optimization and flexible ranking across coupled design modules. The selected hybrid strategy therefore balances physical plausibility, computational efficiency, and interpretability. A linear baseline is retained to show what can be explained by simple additive relationships, while SVR and tree ensembles serve as stronger non-linear comparators. This combination is appropriate because polymer design datasets are often tabular, moderately sized, and enriched by expert-derived descriptors rather than raw sequences or images.

## 4. Experiments
The hydrolysis benchmark used an 80/20 train-test split and 5-fold cross-validation. Evaluation metrics included mean R² ± SD across folds, 95% confidence intervals, test RMSE, and test MAE. For pairwise model comparisons, paired t-tests were performed on fold-level R² values and Holm correction was applied across the three comparisons against the best model. Effect size was reported as paired-sample Cohen’s dz. This was necessary because the data-analysis phase requires effect sizes and uncertainty intervals rather than p-values alone.

The mechanical tradeoff module was evaluated by inspecting the size and structure of the Pareto front obtained from 1000 sampled candidates. The enzyme module was evaluated descriptively by time-course degradation and by comparison of terminal degraded fraction. The ocean simulation used a 365-day horizon with deterministic trajectories for all polymer-zone combinations and Monte Carlo uncertainty quantification for surface-zone PHA. The combinatorial design module ranked top-five compositions per system, and the case-study module compared 54 intervention scenarios. Sensitivity analysis was conducted across five random seeds (7, 13, 21, 42, 84) and with a ±20% perturbation of the SVR regularization parameter. This sensitivity phase was designed to test whether the reported hydrolysis performance is a stable property of the framework rather than an artifact of one random split or one hyperparameter setting.

## 5. Results
The hydrolysis benchmark produced strong predictive performance across all four regressors. SVR was the best overall model with cross-validated R² = 0.925 ± 0.037 and 95% CI [0.878, 0.971]. Linear Regression remained close behind at 0.924 ± 0.025, showing that the noisy mechanistic prior captured much of the governing chemistry. Gradient Boosting and Random Forest yielded 0.901 ± 0.030 and 0.893 ± 0.044, respectively. Fold-wise comparisons showed only modest practical differences once the prior was included. The paired mean R² advantage of SVR over Random Forest was small in absolute magnitude, reinforcing the conclusion that prior-informed descriptors flatten the performance gap between model classes.

The mechanical-design surrogate search identified 39 Pareto-optimal candidates among 1000 random formulations. A representative high-performance Pareto solution combined crystallinity = 0.704, comonomer ratio = 0.289, tensile strength = 64.69 MPa, degradation rate = 0.048 1/day, and mechanical score = 1.336. The Pareto front shows a clear engineering compromise: candidates with the highest degradation rates occupy the low-strength region, whereas the strongest candidates accept substantially slower degradation. The associated heatmap indicates that crystallinity and comonomer ratio interact nonlinearly, with intermediate comonomer levels moderating the typical stiffness-loss penalty.

![Figure 2](figures/fig2_pareto_tradeoff.png)

The enzyme module showed that Cutinase was the strongest option for PHA under the selected conditions, reaching 56.58% degraded fraction by day 30 and setting the overall framework maximum at 56.58%. Lipase and Esterase remained competitive at intermediate substrate concentrations, while PETase was limited by its lower effective Vmax despite favorable affinity. Temperature-response curves showed that Esterase and Cutinase maintained stronger activity over 20–40 °C than PETase, while pH-response curves concentrated activity around near-neutral to mildly alkaline conditions.

![Figure 3](figures/fig3_michaelis_menten.png)

The marine-environment analysis predicted a strong zone dependence. Surface and coastal conditions yielded the most rapid mass loss, whereas deep-sea conditions strongly suppressed degradation because of low temperature and low microbial activity. For surface-zone PHA, the 365-day mass-loss estimate was 99.9999996897641% with 95% CI [99.9999971352870, 99.9999999999966]. The confidence interval is narrow near the endpoint, but the Monte Carlo bands widen earlier in the trajectory, implying that environmental variability affects the timing of mass-loss onset more than the terminal ranking of polymers.

![Figure 4](figures/fig4_ocean_simulation.png)

The combinatorial design module identified PHA-co-PBS at polymer-A fraction 1.00 as the highest-desirability composition with score 0.800. Within mixed systems, PLA-co-PHA around x ≈ 0.40 offered an especially attractive balance between tensile strength (≈41.60 MPa) and degradation rate (≈0.056 1/day), while PLA-co-PBS around x ≈ 0.30 moved Tg closer to room temperature with moderate strength retention. These rankings illustrate that copolymer design can tune degradation and thermo-mechanical behavior simultaneously rather than treating them as independent targets.

![Figure 5](figures/fig5_combinatorial.png)

Case studies across 54 interventions showed that crystallinity reduction was generally the most efficient strategy for accelerating degradation. For PHA, reducing crystallinity to 0.05 produced k = 0.096 1/day with modification efficiency 0.0060. Comparable best crystallinity-reduction points were observed for PLA (k = 0.072 1/day) and PBS (k = 0.084 1/day). By contrast, molecular-weight reduction increased degradation more slowly relative to tensile loss, and copolymerization increased degradation progressively but at the cost of continued strength decline.

![Figure 6](figures/fig6_case_studies.png)

Sensitivity analysis supported the stability of the overall conclusions. Across five seeds, hydrolysis performance, top desirability score, and one-year surface PHA mass loss remained in narrow bands, whereas Pareto-front size varied more substantially because random candidate generation changes the density of non-dominated points. Hyperparameter perturbation of SVR C by ±20% produced only limited changes in mean R², supporting the conclusion that the hydrolysis benchmark is not overly sensitive to one tuned setting.

## 6. Discussion
The most important implication of this study is methodological: hybridizing mechanistic structure with lightweight machine learning can move biodegradable-polymer research beyond binary screening toward rate-aware design. Fransen et al. (2023) showed that classification-based discovery can exceed 82% accuracy for polyester biodegradability, but the present framework suggests that predictive modeling of continuous degradation rates is also feasible with competitive fidelity when physically informed auxiliary descriptors are available. This is important because real design decisions depend on timing and context, not only class labels.

The framework also makes it easier to compare multiple intervention types on a common scale. The case-study analysis showed that reducing crystallinity is typically more efficient than indiscriminate molecular-weight reduction. That insight aligns with polymer-structure arguments discussed by Kim et al. (2023), Samir et al. (2022), and Barletta et al. (2022), yet here it becomes operationally actionable because it is embedded in a direct degradation-versus-strength calculation. Likewise, the enzyme and ocean modules connect biochemical and environmental reasoning more tightly than proxy-only marine models (Min et al., 2020) or isolated enzyme reviews (Tournier et al., 2023; Orlando et al., 2023). Rather than interpreting polymer fate from one descriptor family at a time, the present workflow invites integrated scenario analysis.

At the same time, the results should be interpreted cautiously. Near-complete one-year surface PHA mass loss is a model-based estimate under simplified assumptions and should not be read as a universal field prediction. Similarly, the top combinatorial candidate being strongly PHA-like demonstrates what the chosen desirability function rewards; alternative industrial objectives could easily favor different candidates. Thus, the framework is best viewed as a transparent hypothesis-generation tool and not as a substitute for controlled degradation testing.

## 7. Limitations and Future Work
### Data Limitations
This framework is based primarily on synthetic data that were generated from physically plausible but simplified equations. That choice was appropriate for a rapid proof-of-concept and allowed all six modules to be linked coherently, but it also imposes an obvious ceiling on evidentiary strength. The hydrolysis model, tradeoff model, and combinatorial design module do not yet incorporate the full heterogeneity of real polymer datasets, including assay-to-assay noise, batch effects, processing-history dependence, and vendor-specific resin variation. Sample sizes are therefore only representative of a computational benchmark, not of an industrial-scale design database.

### Methodological Limitations
Several modules intentionally use reduced-order models. The hydrolysis predictor uses a noisy mechanistic prior and a limited descriptor set. The enzyme module relies on classical Michaelis-Menten kinetics rather than adsorption-limited or deactivation-aware forms. The ocean module collapses environmental complexity into temperature, pH, microbial activity, and UV terms, leaving out salinity, biofilm formation, hydrodynamics, and fragmentation. The Pareto search uses random sampling rather than a full evolutionary optimizer or constrained Bayesian optimization. These simplifications keep the framework transparent and lightweight, but they also mean that some nonlinearities and real-world feedback loops are omitted.

### Evaluation Limitations
The central performance benchmark is a synthetic hydrolysis dataset with internal cross-validation. External validation with independent real-world datasets is essential to confirm the generalizability of these findings beyond simulated conditions. Only one explicit mechanistic prior and four standard machine-learning baselines were evaluated. Although this is sufficient to demonstrate the framework concept, it does not establish that the selected models dominate Gaussian processes, graph neural networks, Bayesian hierarchical models, or other advanced alternatives. Nor does the current study quantify laboratory uncertainty in tensile testing, thermal transitions, or enzymatic assay reproducibility.

### Generalizability
The present framework focuses on PLA, PHA, and PBS and on a narrow set of environmental scenarios. Transfer to aromatic-aliphatic copolyesters, multilayer packaging materials, filled composites, or biomedical implants will require reparameterization and possibly different governing equations. Domain shift is particularly relevant when moving from controlled hydrolysis conditions to mixed environmental exposures or microbiologically complex ecosystems.

### Future Directions
Over the next 6 months, the most valuable extension would be calibration against curated experimental degradation datasets and enzyme-assay panels, together with broader tensile and thermal measurements. Over a 1–2 year horizon, the framework could be expanded toward Bayesian design loops, life-cycle constraints, cost-aware objective functions, and experimentally grounded marine-weathering models. Additional short-term work should also compare the current random Pareto search with more sample-efficient optimizers. These directions would transform the present framework from a proof-of-concept analysis environment into a stronger decision-support platform for sustainable polymer design.

## 8. Conclusion
This study presented a reproducible computational framework that connects hydrolysis kinetics, enzyme-mediated degradation, marine-environment simulation, mechanical-property tradeoffs, combinatorial composition search, and polymer-specific case studies in one workflow. The results show that physics-informed machine learning can predict hydrolysis rates with strong fidelity, that Cutinase-like conditions are especially favorable for PHA degradation, and that crystallinity reduction is often a more efficient design lever than indiscriminate molecular-weight reduction. The framework does not replace experiments, but it offers a structured and computationally efficient way to prioritize experiments, compare design hypotheses, and reason across multiple degradation contexts. In that sense, it helps close the gap between isolated biodegradability metrics and actionable molecular design.
