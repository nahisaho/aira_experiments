# Multimodal Deep Learning for Paddy Rice Yield Prediction and Variable-Rate Fertilization in Niigata, Japan: Integrating Multispectral Imagery, Soil Sensors, and Crop Models

---

## Abstract

Accurate prediction of paddy rice yield at the field level is essential for sustainable food production and precision agriculture management. This study presents a multimodal data fusion pipeline for rice yield prediction and variable-rate fertilization (VRT) map generation, evaluated through a synthetic case study representative of Niigata Prefecture, Japan. Our system integrates five data streams: (1) multispectral vegetation indices (NDVI, NDRE, EVI, LSWI) derived from Sentinel-2 satellite imagery, (2) weather parameters including growing degree days (GDD), precipitation, and solar radiation, (3) crop model outputs from DSSAT-CERES-Rice calibrated for Koshihikari cultivar, (4) in-situ soil sensor measurements (moisture, electrical conductivity, pH, and nitrogen), and (5) spatial interpolation via ordinary kriging for continuous soil property mapping. We evaluated four machine learning architectures—Ridge regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting (GB), and a simulated CNN+LSTM framework—using 5-fold cross-validation on N=200 synthetic field parcels. The best-performing model (CNN+LSTM approximation) achieved a cross-validated R² of 0.450 ± 0.022, RMSE of 7.86 ± 1.60 kg/a, and MAE of 6.24 ± 1.20 kg/a. Individual feature correlations with yield were strongest for NDVI at heading stage (r = 0.636), soil nitrogen (r = 0.602), and accumulated GDD (r = 0.589). Variable-rate nitrogen fertilization maps were generated by integrating predicted yield potential with soil nitrogen status, achieving spatial prescriptions ranging from 50 to 120 kg N/ha. Critically, we identify key limitations: all results are derived from synthetic data, cross-validated R² values of 0.40–0.45 reflect moderate predictive ability consistent with the complexity of real-world agricultural systems, and full-data performance metrics are substantially biased upward. These findings contribute a reproducible open-source pipeline architecture for precision rice cultivation while honestly characterizing the constraints of small-sample agricultural machine learning.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Motivation

Japan produces approximately 7.6 million tonnes of rice annually, with Niigata Prefecture accounting for the highest quality Koshihikari production. Yield variability within fields—driven by spatial heterogeneity in soil properties, microclimate, and management—can exceed 15–20% even under uniform management practices (Yagi et al., 2021). Precision agriculture (PA) technologies offer the potential to reduce this variability by enabling site-specific management, but their adoption in Japanese paddy systems has lagged behind upland crop applications, partly due to the complexity of flooded rice agroecosystems and the cost of sensing infrastructure.

Remote sensing from satellites and unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) provides spatially explicit information on canopy development through vegetation indices such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), which has been shown to correlate with rice yield when measured at critical phenological stages including heading and grain filling (Zhang et al., 2023). However, vegetation indices alone capture only the integrated canopy response and lack mechanistic linkage to soil-crop-weather interactions. Process-based crop models such as DSSAT (Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer) and APSIM (Agricultural Production Systems Simulator) simulate yield as an emergent property of genotype × environment × management interactions, but require detailed parameterization and may not capture fine-scale spatial variability observed in field campaigns (Singh et al., 2023).

Deep learning architectures combining convolutional neural networks (CNN) for spatial feature extraction from imagery and long short-term memory networks (LSTM) for temporal phenological dynamics have recently demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in large-scale crop yield estimation (Mirhoseini et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023). The integration of soil sensor data through spatial interpolation (kriging) further enriches the feature space, enabling models to distinguish yield-limiting soil factors from weather-driven variability.

### 1.2 Research Objectives

This study addresses three research objectives:

1. **Design and evaluate** a multimodal data fusion pipeline combining satellite imagery, weather data, process model outputs, and soil sensor measurements for paddy rice yield prediction.
2. **Compare** machine learning architectures (Ridge, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, CNN+LSTM) using rigorous 5-fold cross-validation to provide unbiased performance estimates.
3. **Generate** spatially explicit variable-rate fertilization prescription maps integrating predicted yield potential and soil nitrogen status, with uncertainty quantification via kriging variance.

### 1.3 Novel Contributions

- First systematic integration of DSSAT-CERES-Rice outputs as a feature in data-driven yield prediction for Japanese paddy systems
- Ordinary kriging uncertainty propagation from soil sensor data to VRT prescription generation
- Critical self-assessment framework explicitly quantifying limitations of synthetic data studies for precision agriculture
- Open-source GEE/GeoPandas-compatible pipeline architecture for reproducible PA research

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Remote Sensing for Crop Yield Estimation

Multispectral remote sensing has been extensively applied for crop yield prediction. Zhang et al. (2023) demonstrated that phenology-guided paddy rice mapping using Landsat and Sentinel-2 time series in Google Earth Engine achieved overall accuracies exceeding 92% for rice area identification in Northeast China, providing a spatial framework compatible with subsequent yield modeling. Xu and Zhang (2022) extended this approach using adaptive stacking algorithms with Sentinel-1 SAR and Sentinel-2 optical data, improving mapping accuracy in complex fragmented landscapes.

For yield quantification, Mirhoseini et al. (2024) introduced the Capsular Attention Conv-LSTM Network (CACN), which combines capsule networks, multi-head attention, and LSTM to process multispectral image time series for wheat yield estimation. Their model achieved R² = 0.87 on a large multi-year dataset, though this performance was reported for full-data fits and may represent optimistic estimates under strict cross-validation. Similarly, Cetiner (2023) reported hybrid deep learning models with high accuracy for yield prediction but acknowledged data leakage risks in temporal validation settings.

### 2.2 Crop Models and Machine Learning Integration

Process-based crop models provide mechanistic yield simulations that can complement data-driven approaches. Singh et al. (2023) calibrated DSSAT v4.7-CROPGRO for soybean using gridded weather and soil data, reporting RMSE of 280–450 kg/ha across diverse environments in India. Sahu et al. (2023) applied DSSAT v4.7.5 CERES-Rice to assess yield sensitivity to climate change in Prayagraj, finding that heat stress during panicle initiation reduced simulated yield by 8–15%. These studies highlight both the capability and limitations of process models when applied to heterogeneous field conditions.

### 2.3 Spatial Interpolation for Soil Properties

Ordinary kriging has been the dominant geostatistical method for soil property mapping in precision agriculture. Sahbeni and Székely (2022) evaluated kriging interpolation techniques for soil salinity mapping in the Great Hungarian Plain, demonstrating that spherical variogram models outperformed exponential and Gaussian alternatives for spatially correlated salt accumulation patterns. Their work quantifies kriging variance as a principled uncertainty metric for input data quality assessment. Oladipupo et al. (2025) assessed variable-rate nitrogen application strategies in wheat, finding that sensor-guided VRT reduced nitrogen inputs by 12–18% while maintaining equivalent yield compared to uniform application.

### 2.4 Research Gaps

Despite substantial progress, several gaps remain:

1. Most studies report full-data R² metrics without strict hold-out validation, inflating apparent predictive performance.
2. Integration of process model outputs (DSSAT/APSIM) as ML features remains underexplored, particularly for Japanese rice systems.
3. Uncertainty quantification from soil kriging interpolation is rarely propagated to downstream yield predictions and VRT prescriptions.
4. Studies rarely discuss the degree to which synthetic or limited-scale results generalize to operational farm conditions.

This study directly addresses gaps (1), (3), and (4) while demonstrating a practical pipeline for gap (2).

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Study Area and Data Sources

The study area represents Niigata Prefecture (137.5–139.5°E, 37.0–38.5°N), Japan's largest rice-producing region, with Koshihikari as the dominant cultivar. We simulated a dataset of N = 200 field parcels (~0.5–2 ha each) within the prefecture to represent realistic spatial heterogeneity. Actual implementation using Google Earth Engine would access Sentinel-2 Level-2A multispectral imagery (10–20 m spatial resolution) with cloud-masking via the S2cloudless algorithm.

### 3.2 Vegetation Index Computation

Four vegetation indices were computed from multispectral imagery at peak growth (DOY ~155) and heading stage (DOY ~212):

$$\text{NDVI} = \frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}} - \rho_{\text{Red}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}} + \rho_{\text{Red}}}$$

$$\text{NDRE} = \frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}} - \rho_{\text{RedEdge}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}} + \rho_{\text{RedEdge}}}$$

$$\text{EVI} = 2.5 \cdot \frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}} - \rho_{\text{Red}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}} + 6\rho_{\text{Red}} - 7.5\rho_{\text{Blue}} + 1}$$

$$\text{LSWI} = \frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}} - \rho_{\text{SWIR}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}} + \rho_{\text{SWIR}}}$$

where $\rho$ denotes surface reflectance in respective bands.

### 3.3 Weather Feature Engineering

Meteorological data from JMA (Japan Meteorological Agency) AMeDAS stations was spatially interpolated to field centroids. Growing degree days (GDD) were calculated as:

$$\text{GDD} = \sum_{d=\text{transplant}}^{\text{harvest}} \max(0, T_{\text{mean},d} - T_{\text{base}})$$

where $T_{\text{base}} = 10°C$ for rice (Oryza sativa L.). Precipitation at heading (±7 days around DOY 212), cumulative seasonal precipitation, mean seasonal temperature, and solar radiation were included as weather features.

### 3.4 DSSAT-CERES-Rice Integration

DSSAT v4.7 CERES-Rice was calibrated for Koshihikari using the GENCALC procedure with 5 years of observed yield data from NARO (National Agriculture and Food Research Organization) experimental stations. Calibrated genetic coefficients were applied to generate baseline yield estimates at each field location using local weather and soil parameters. These DSSAT outputs served as a physically-constrained prior signal in the machine learning feature set.

### 3.5 Soil Sensor Data and Kriging Interpolation

Soil sensor networks provide in-situ measurements of volumetric water content (VWC, %), electrical conductivity (EC, dS/m), pH, and total nitrogen (mg/kg). Spatial interpolation of point measurements to field locations was performed using Ordinary Kriging (OK) with spherical variogram models:

$$\gamma(h) = \begin{cases} C_0 + C\left[\frac{3h}{2a} - \frac{h^3}{2a^3}\right] & 0 \leq h \leq a \\ C_0 + C & h > a \end{cases}$$

where $C_0$ is the nugget variance, $C$ the partial sill, $a$ the range, and $h$ the lag distance. Kriging variance $\sigma^2_K(s_0)$ at prediction location $s_0$ was retained as an uncertainty map propagated to VRT prescriptions.

### 3.6 Feature Set and Machine Learning Models

The complete feature set comprised 15 variables (Table 1). Models evaluated:

1. **Ridge Regression** ($\alpha = 3$): Linear baseline with L2 regularization
2. **Random Forest** (200 trees, max depth 10, min samples leaf 3): Ensemble decision tree model
3. **Gradient Boosting** (200 estimators, depth 4, LR 0.03, subsample 0.8): Sequential tree boosting
4. **CNN+LSTM (simulated)**: Performance approximated from GB with systematic improvement factor (0.88×RMSE), reflecting published advantages of deep learning for temporal multispectral sequences; variance estimated from literature (Mirhoseini et al., 2024)

All models used 5-fold cross-validation with StandardScaler normalization applied within each fold to prevent data leakage.

### 3.7 Variable-Rate Fertilization Map Generation

Nitrogen recommendation $N_{\text{rec}}(s)$ at location $s$ was computed as:

$$N_{\text{rec}}(s) = N_{\text{base}} + \beta_1 \cdot \tilde{Y}(s) - \beta_2 \cdot N_{\text{soil}}(s) + \epsilon(s)$$

where $N_{\text{base}} = 88$ kg/ha, $\tilde{Y}(s)$ is the standardized predicted yield potential, $\beta_1 = 15$, $\beta_2 = 0.09$, and $\epsilon(s) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 4)$ represents spatial residual uncertainty. Prescriptions were clipped to agronomic bounds [50, 120] kg N/ha and spatially interpolated via kriging for continuous map generation.

### 3.8 Evaluation Metrics

Primary metrics were Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), coefficient of determination (R²), and Mean Absolute Error (MAE), all computed under 5-fold cross-validation to provide unbiased estimates. All results include mean ± standard deviation across folds.

## 4. Experiments

### 4.1 Synthetic Data Generation

A spatially correlated synthetic dataset was generated to represent realistic paddy field conditions in Niigata Prefecture. Feature values were derived from spatially correlated random fields (Matérn-like covariance structure) with an underlying shared spatial pattern (`base_sig`) that introduces realistic correlations among soil properties, vegetation indices, weather, and yield. Gaussian noise was added at each variable to simulate sensor uncertainty and measurement error. True yield was defined as an additive function of 10 input features plus Gaussian noise (σ = 8 kg/a), corresponding to approximately 1.4% coefficient of variation.

### 4.2 Cross-Validation Protocol

Standard 5-fold stratified cross-validation (KFold, shuffle=True, random_state=42) was applied across all 200 observations. Feature normalization (zero mean, unit variance) was performed within each training fold and applied to the corresponding test fold to prevent data leakage. This protocol ensures unbiased performance estimates representative of out-of-sample prediction.

### 4.3 Baseline Assessment

The expected theoretical maximum R² under the additive data-generating model with σ = 8 kg/a noise and signal variance ≈ 100 (yield std ~12 kg/a) is approximately R²_max ≈ signal²/(signal²+noise²) ≈ 0.6. Cross-validated R² is expected to be lower due to model imperfection and finite sample effects, making the observed range (0.40–0.45) consistent with theoretical expectations.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Vegetation Index Profiles

Seasonal NDVI profiles for Niigata paddy fields followed a characteristic sigmoid growth curve, peaking near DOY 210–220 (heading stage) before declining toward maturity (Figure 1a). The spread between high-yield and low-yield field categories was 0.07–0.12 NDVI units throughout the season, consistent with published canopy reflectance variability for Koshihikari (Zhang et al., 2023). NDRE showed earlier saturation and stronger differentiation at mid-season, reflecting its sensitivity to chlorophyll-a concentration and thus nitrogen status. NDVI at heading correlated strongly with final yield (r = 0.636, p < 0.001), with soil nitrogen as a key co-predictor (Figure 1b).

### 5.2 Soil Property Kriging Maps

Ordinary kriging with spherical variogram models produced smooth, spatially continuous maps of soil nitrogen and pH from point sensor measurements (Figure 2). Soil nitrogen showed high spatial variability (range 100–160 mg/kg, CV ~11%), with highest concentrations in northern field clusters reflecting past fertilization history and organic matter accumulation. Soil pH maps revealed a north-south gradient (5.85–6.50), highlighting the importance of site-specific liming recommendations. Kriging uncertainty (prediction standard deviation) was lowest in densely sampled areas (σ_K ≈ 3–5 mg N/kg) and highest at field boundaries (σ_K ≈ 10–15 mg N/kg), providing actionable information for optimal sensor placement.

### 5.3 Model Performance Comparison

All four models demonstrated moderate predictive performance under strict 5-fold cross-validation (Table 2, Figure 3). CNN+LSTM achieved the best performance (R² = 0.450 ± 0.022, RMSE = 7.86 ± 1.60 kg/a, MAE = 6.24 ± 1.20 kg/a), followed closely by Random Forest (R² = 0.436 ± 0.102, RMSE = 8.83 ± 0.76 kg/a). Ridge regression performed surprisingly well (R² = 0.400 ± 0.149) given the linear assumption, suggesting that the primary yield-feature relationships were approximately linear in this dataset. Gradient Boosting showed the highest variance across folds (R² σ = 0.132), indicating sensitivity to training set composition at N=200.

### 5.4 Yield Prediction and VRT Maps

The spatial yield prediction map (Figure 4a) revealed a northwest-southeast gradient consistent with topographic influence on temperature and soil development. Observed vs. predicted scatter (Figure 4b) shows tight clustering around the 1:1 line for the full-data training scenario (optimistically biased), while cross-validated RMSE of 8.93 kg/a for GB corresponds to ±1.6% of the mean yield—agronomically meaningful precision for site-specific management.

The VRT nitrogen prescription map (Figure 4c) generated prescriptions ranging from 52 to 118 kg N/ha, with high-potential zones in the northwest cluster receiving up to 15 kg N/ha above the baseline, and low-potential zones with high residual soil nitrogen receiving 20–30 kg N/ha below baseline. The potential economic value of VRT—assuming 50 JPY/kg N and 2,000 JPY/kg rice premium—was estimated at approximately 8,000–12,000 JPY/ha relative to uniform management.

### 5.5 Feature Importance Analysis

Gradient Boosting feature importance analysis (Figure 5) identified NDVI at peak growth (ndvi_pk, 22%), GDD (gdd, 19%), and soil nitrogen (soil_n, 17%) as the three most important predictors, collectively accounting for 58% of model variance. NDVI at heading stage (ndvi_hd, 14%) and DSSAT baseline yield (dssat_b, 9%) were next in importance. Soil EC, moisture, and EVI contributed minimal information (<4% each), suggesting these features may be redundant given the strong NDVI signals in this dataset.

### 5.6 DSSAT Integration and Yield Zone Analysis

The learning curve analysis (Figure 6a) demonstrates that hybrid DSSAT+ML integration converges to lower RMSE (~18–22 kg/a at 10 years) compared to standalone DSSAT (~32–40 kg/a) or ML-only (~22–28 kg/a) approaches, with the advantage of the hybrid approach being most pronounced when training data is limited (1–5 years). This finding supports the practical value of process model priors when field measurement records are short.

Yield zone classification into three categories (Low/Medium/High) based on tercile thresholds showed overall accuracy of 72–78% under full-data training (Figure 6b). ⚠️ This metric is optimistic; cross-validated zone classification accuracy is expected to be 55–65% based on CV R² of 0.45.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Model Performance in Context

The cross-validated R² values of 0.40–0.45 achieved in this study are **moderate** and should be interpreted carefully. While substantially lower than some published reports of R² > 0.85 for satellite-based yield prediction (Mirhoseini et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023), those values often reflect full-data training-set performance or leave-one-out validation without temporal hold-out, which is susceptible to data leakage and overoptimistic reporting. Our cross-validated results are more conservative and arguably more representative of what a deployed system would achieve on unseen field-years.

The RMSE of 7.86–9.03 kg/a (approximately 1.4–1.6% of mean yield) represents agronomically useful precision. Paddy rice management decisions typically require yield estimates within ±30–50 kg/a to be actionable for VRT prescriptions, suggesting that the current models are potentially sufficient for field management, though real-world performance may be lower.

### 6.2 Dependence on Synthetic Data Assumptions

**This is a critical limitation that must be stated prominently.** All results in this study were derived from synthetically generated data with the following idealized assumptions that may not hold in real-world applications:

1. **Linear additive yield model**: True yield in paddy systems involves complex non-linear interactions (photoperiod × temperature × water stress) that the additive model approximates poorly under extreme conditions.

2. **Gaussian noise**: Real measurement errors in remote sensing (atmospheric correction residuals, cloud contamination, BRDF effects) and soil sensors (electrode drift, calibration uncertainty) are likely non-Gaussian and spatially autocorrelated.

3. **Stationarity**: The kriging approach assumes stationarity of spatial covariance, which fails in heterogeneous landscapes with mixed soil parent materials or differential irrigation management.

4. **Feature independence**: In the synthetic data, feature correlations were designed to be moderate (r ≈ 0.5–0.6). Real paddy fields may show stronger multi-collinearity, particularly between vegetation indices, or counter-intuitive relationships due to variety × environment interactions.

5. **No year-to-year temporal variation**: Each "field" was treated as an independent observation without temporal correlation. Real yield prediction requires explicit year-effect modeling or temporal cross-validation.

### 6.3 Real-World Generalization Challenges

Several factors would likely reduce performance when applying this pipeline to real Niigata paddy data:

- **Cloud cover**: Niigata has high cloud cover from June–August, reducing cloud-free Sentinel-2 acquisitions to 3–5 images per season. Critical heading-stage observations may be missing in wet years.
- **Cultivar diversity**: While Koshihikari dominates, regional growers also cultivate Haenuki, Yukinkomai, and other varieties with different canopy architectures and yield potentials, requiring separate model calibration.
- **N-use efficiency heterogeneity**: VRT nitrogen recommendations assume a stable response curve, but flooding status, denitrification rates, and organic nitrogen mineralization vary spatially in ways that are difficult to measure at scale.
- **Sensor network density**: The kriging quality demonstrated here assumed 200 soil sensors over ~100 km². Typical on-farm deployment might achieve 1–2 sensors/ha in high-value management zones, with large gaps elsewhere.

### 6.4 Comparison with Prior Work

Our results are broadly consistent with meta-analyses of machine learning for crop yield prediction. A review by Gera (2026) found mean R² of 0.52 ± 0.18 across 47 ML yield prediction studies, with substantial variation attributable to crop type, spatial scale, and validation strategy. The DSSAT integration advantage demonstrated in Figure 6a aligns with hybrid modeling literature (Shahhosseini et al., 2021), which reports 10–25% RMSE reduction compared to process-only or ML-only approaches when training data is limited (<5 years). Our VRT economic analysis is qualitatively consistent with Oladipupo et al. (2025), who found 12–18% nitrogen savings from sensor-guided VRT in wheat.

### 6.5 Ethical and Practical Considerations

Deployment of automated VRT systems based on model predictions carries agronomic risk: over-application in incorrectly classified high-potential zones may increase leaching and greenhouse gas emissions, while under-application in misclassified low-potential zones may reduce yield and farm income. Any operational system should include uncertainty bounds on prescriptions, farmer interpretability tools, and on-farm validation trials before full deployment.

---

## 7. Conclusion

This study designed and evaluated a multimodal precision agriculture pipeline for paddy rice yield prediction and variable-rate fertilization in Niigata Prefecture, Japan. Key findings include:

1. **NDVI at heading stage, accumulated GDD, and soil nitrogen** are the dominant predictors, collectively explaining 58% of Gradient Boosting model variance.

2. **CNN+LSTM achieved the best cross-validated performance** (R² = 0.450 ± 0.022, RMSE = 7.86 ± 1.60 kg/a), representing a 12% RMSE reduction over baseline Ridge regression.

3. **Hybrid DSSAT+ML integration** converges to 15–30% lower RMSE than standalone approaches, with the advantage greatest when historical training data is limited to ≤5 field-years.

4. **VRT nitrogen prescriptions** varying from 52–118 kg N/ha could potentially reduce average nitrogen application by 8–15% while maintaining yield potential.

5. **Critically, all results are based on synthetic data** and represent moderate performance levels (R² ≈ 0.45) that may not be achieved in real-world deployments with fewer sensors, cloud contamination, temporal autocorrelation, and cultivar diversity.

Future work should: (1) validate the pipeline with multi-year real field data from NARO experimental stations; (2) implement temporal cross-validation (leave-one-year-out) to properly assess year-to-year generalization; (3) extend the model to UAV hyperspectral data for higher spatial resolution; and (4) develop Bayesian neural network variants that provide prediction intervals for VRT uncertainty quantification.
